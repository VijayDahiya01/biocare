The problem

Knowing who actually walked in is still guesswork.

Every method in use today breaks in a predictable way.

Cards get shared

One card, many people. A friend punches you in. Nobody can prove otherwise.

Fingerprints are slow

A queue at 9am, wet or worn fingers fail, and everyone touches the same pad.

Visitor books are useless

In a fire, a paper register cannot tell you who is still inside the building.

Re-enrol everywhere

Your office, your gym, your child's school — each one scans your face all over again.

And now it's a legal problem, not just an operational one.

India's DPDP Act treats a face as sensitive personal data. Most attendance systems store the actual photograph — which means consent, deletion and proof are now obligations they cannot meet.

The idea

Your face becomes your badge —
and you decide where it works.

You verify yourself one time. After that, joining a new place is a single tap — because the identity already exists and you grant permission to each place.

How it works

Four steps. The first one happens only once.

Steps 3 and 4 are what a person does every day. They take about two seconds.

01

Agree, then scan

You tick four plain-language boxes — what it's for, that it's sensitive, what your rights are, and that nobody forced you. Then the camera captures your face.

02

Join a place

Your office invites you. You tap allow. No second scan — your existing identity is reused, with your permission, for that one place.

03

Walk up and look

At the gate, the camera sees you and matches you. The screen says Welcome — or explains why not.

04

The business sees it

Who is inside right now, who came late, who was refused and why — updating live on a dashboard.

For the organisation

The questions a manager actually asks.

“Who is inside right now?”

A live count, by building and by zone. In a fire drill or a real evacuation, that list is the difference between a headcount and a guess.

“Who was late, and how often?”

Attendance that nobody has to type in, tally, or argue about — because the gate recorded it.

“Tell me when something's wrong.”

A barred person at the gate. Someone entering a restricted area. A child who hasn't arrived by the cut-off — the parent gets a message.

“Prove it to an auditor.”

Every entry, consent and deletion is written to a record that cannot be quietly edited afterwards.

The law

Built for DPDP from the first line, not patched on later.

India's data-protection law asks four things of anyone handling a face. Here's how each is answered.

Consent before capture

Four plain-language tick-boxes, recorded with a timestamp. No consent, no scan — the system physically refuses to enrol.

Minimum data kept

The photograph is destroyed at capture. Only the number-string survives, encrypted.

Erasure on request

One tap removes your face from one place — or from everything, everywhere, with a certificate confirming it's gone.

A way to complain

A built-in grievance channel, because the law requires a named route for people to raise problems.

For children, consent belongs to a parent or guardian — and there is always a manual lane for families who decline, so nobody is shut out for saying no.
