Part 1
Introduction & How Everything Works

1. Introduction
BioCore is a web-based face authentication and attendance platform. It lets people prove who they are with a selfie instead of cards, PINs, fingerprints, or paper registers. It is built on ZepIris, an open-source face authentication engine, and is designed to run at operational scale on ordinary hardware — a webcam, a tablet, or an IP camera is enough.
This document is the single source of truth for the platform. It is written for four audiences at once: developers who build and maintain it, the sales team who position and sell it, the onboarding team who set up new clients, and the client administrators who run it day to day. Where a section is specific to one audience, it is marked.
What this platform replaces
Paper attendance registers and manual muster rolls.
Punch cards, RFID cards, and PIN keypads that can be shared or lost.
Fingerprint scanners that fail with dust, sweat, or worn prints.
“Buddy punching” — one person clocking in on behalf of another.

1.1 The core idea in one paragraph
A person enrolls their face once. From then on, they walk up to any shared terminal (a “kiosk”), look at the camera, and the system recognises them in under two seconds and records a check-in or check-out. The organisation administrator watches all of this live from a web dashboard, runs reports, and configures the system — but never has to physically operate a device. Everything is web-based; there is no mobile app to install.
1.2 The three actors
Every deployment, in every industry, is built around the same three actors. Understanding these three is the key to understanding the whole platform.

1.3 The end-to-end flow
Here is exactly what happens, in order, across the life of a deployment:
Onboarding — the entity (a school, hotel, factory) is set up on the platform with its own isolated tenant, branding, and configuration.
Registration — each person enrolls their face, either by themselves (self-registration) or with help from an admin (admin-assisted enrollment).
Daily use — people check in and out at shared kiosks throughout the day. Each scan is matched, recorded, and pushed live to the dashboard.
Monitoring — the admin watches live attendance, gets alerts (late arrival, unrecognised face, device offline), and runs reports.
Administration — the admin manages users, creates zones and badges, configures devices, exports payroll, and handles DPDP requests (data access, erasure).

Key design principle — the backend never changes
The entire backend (ZepIris ML, databases, storage, queues) is identical for a school, a hotel, a factory, or a hospital.
Only the configuration and a few optional modules differ per industry.
This means one codebase serves all verticals — lower maintenance, faster onboarding, consistent behaviour.

Deep System Architecture

2. System architecture
The platform is built as a set of independent services that each do one job and talk to each other over the network. This is called a microservice architecture. It lets each part scale on its own — for example, the face-matching service can run on more powerful hardware than the rest — and lets the team update one part without touching the others.

2.2 Each service explained
Face authentication service
Handles enrollment and verification. When a face image arrives, it coordinates with the ML inference layer to check image quality and generate a face vector, then stores or searches that vector in the Milvus database. It is the heart of the platform.
Attendance service
Records check-in and check-out events. It applies the “toggle logic” that decides whether a scan is an entry or an exit based on the person's last event. It calculates durations, flags late arrivals, and handles shifts, overtime, and breaks.
Access control service
Decides whether a recognised person is allowed into a particular zone at a particular time, based on their role and the zone's rules. On a positive decision it can trigger a physical door or barrier through the integration layer.
Visitor management service
Handles people who are not permanent members — walk-in visitors, delivery agents, event delegates. It issues temporary, time-limited enrollments through QR codes or links that auto-expire.
HR & payroll service
Turns attendance data into useful HR outputs: leave balances, shift schedules, wage calculations for hourly and daily workers, and payslip-ready exports.
Notification service
Sends alerts through browser notifications, email, SMS, and optionally WhatsApp. It applies priority routing so that critical alerts (a breach, an unrecognised face) go out instantly while routine ones (a successful check-in) are lower priority.
Reporting service
Generates dashboards, scheduled reports, and exports in PDF, Excel, and CSV. It is read-only — it never changes data, only summarises it.
Multi-tenant service
Keeps every client organisation's data completely separate. A school's data is invisible to a hotel on the same platform. It also handles per-tenant branding, subscription plans, and billing.

How a face becomes a match — plain English
1. The camera captures an image and sends it to the platform.
2. ZepIris checks the image is sharp, real (not a photo of a photo), and appropriate.
3. ZepIris converts the face into a list of 512 numbers — a “vector” — that uniquely describes it.
4. Milvus compares that vector against every enrolled vector and returns the closest matches with a score.
5. If the top score is above the threshold (default 0.5), it's a match. The person is identified.

3.2 The administrative roles in detail
Super admin (platform owner)
This is the platform operator — the company that owns and runs BioCore. The super admin can see and manage every client entity, provision new tenants, set global policies, and access platform-wide health and billing. No client ever has this role. It is held only by the platform team.
Sees: all entities, all devices, global metrics, billing.
Can: create / suspend entities, set global config, manage platform staff.
Cannot: read a tenant's biometric data without an explicit, audited support grant.
Entity admin (client organisation)
The top administrator inside one client — the school principal, the hotel general manager, the factory HR head. This person controls everything within their own organisation but can never see another organisation's data.
Sees: all users, attendance, zones, devices, reports, and settings within their entity.
Can: invite managers and staff, create zones and badges, register devices, configure DPDP settings, run admin-assisted enrollment.
Manager / supervisor
A limited administrator scoped to a slice of the organisation — one department, one branch, or one set of zones. A factory floor supervisor sees only their shift; a school's class teacher sees only their class.
Contractor sub-admin
A special administrator for the construction, factory, and logistics verticals. A contractor brings their own crew of workers onto a client's site. The contractor sub-admin manages only their own workers, sees only their own attendance and wage data, but operates under the parent entity's umbrella and rules.
HR / payroll
Sees attendance and wage data and can export payroll, but has no control over devices, zones, or security alerts. This separation means the payroll team gets exactly what it needs and nothing more.
Auditor / DPO (Data Protection Officer)
A strictly read-only role required by the DPDP Act 2026. The auditor or DPO can review every audit log, every consent record, and every data-access event, but cannot change any operational data. This independence is what makes the compliance trail trustworthy.
Security / reception operator
The person at the front desk or security post. They operate kiosks, enroll walk-in visitors, respond to alerts (an unrecognised face, a blacklisted person), but cannot see payroll, reports, or system settings.

3.3 The enrolled-person roles in detail
Self-registered user
The most common type. Employees, students, gym members, and contract workers register themselves on a browser using their organisation code, verify by email OTP, give consent, and capture their own face. No admin involvement is needed. This is the default and the fastest path.
Admin-enrolled person
Some people cannot or should not self-register — a hospital patient, a prison inmate, a citizen at a government counter, a devotee at a temple. For these, an administrator enrolls them in person, capturing their face and recording their consent on their behalf. This is covered in full in the onboarding section.
Guardian
A parent or guardian linked to a student, used by schools for pickup verification. The guardian self-enrolls from home via a secure link sent by the school. At pickup time, the kiosk matches the guardian's face and confirms the student link before the child is released.
Visitor
A temporary, time-limited identity. A visitor receives a QR code or link, captures their face at reception, and is granted access for a fixed window (say four hours). After that, the enrollment automatically expires and the data is deleted. No organisation code is needed.
Kiosk device (non-human)
A kiosk is itself a registered identity — but not a person. The entity admin registers each terminal with a device token so the platform knows which device recorded which event, and so an offline or tampered device can be detected and disabled. The kiosk has no login screen for end users.

Why visitors and kiosks are “users” too
Treating visitors and devices as first-class identities means every action in the system traces back to a known identity — a person or a device.
This is what makes the DPDP audit trail complete: who (or what) did what, when, and from where.

Part 5
Zones, Devices & Badges

5. Zones, devices, and badges
Three building blocks let one platform model any physical environment — a single-room gym or a multi-building hospital campus. These are zones, devices, and badges. This part explains what each is and exactly how to create them.
5.1 Zones
A zone is a named area that access is controlled for. It can be physical (a server room, a hostel, a backstage area) or logical (a department, a shift group). Every kiosk belongs to a zone, and every person's access is defined in terms of zones.
How to create a zone
Open the admin dashboard and go to Settings › Zones.
Click “Add zone” and give it a name (e.g. “ICU”, “Main gate”, “VIP lounge”).
Choose the zone type — entry/exit, restricted, or amenity.
Set the access rule — which roles or badges may enter, and at which times.
Optionally link a physical door controller so a positive match opens the door.
Assign one or more devices (kiosks) to the zone.

Zone access rule — worked example
Zone: “Server room” (restricted).
Rule: only the “IT staff” badge may enter, only between 08:00 and 20:00.
Anyone else who is recognised at the server-room kiosk is denied and an alert is raised.
An unrecognised face triggers an immediate security alert.

5.2 Devices (kiosks)
A device is a registered terminal that runs the check-in screen. It can be a tablet, a desktop with a webcam, or an IP camera feeding a server. Each device is registered once and then runs unattended.
How to register a device
Go to Settings › Devices and click “Register device”.
Name the device (e.g. “KIOSK-GATE-01”) and assign it to a zone.
Choose the capture method — browser webcam or IP camera (RTSP).
The platform issues a device token; open the kiosk URL on that terminal and it pairs automatically.
Put the terminal into full-screen kiosk mode so it cannot be navigated away from.

Device health & security
Each device reports online/offline status to the dashboard in real time.
If a device goes offline or is tampered with, the admin is alerted and the device can be remotely disabled.

5.3 Badges
A badge is an access level — a named bundle of permissions that says which zones a person may enter and when. Instead of setting permissions person by person, you assign a badge, and everyone with that badge inherits its rules. Badges are the simplest way to manage access at scale.
How to create a badge
Go to Settings › Badges and click “Create badge”.
Name it (e.g. “Doctor”, “VIP guest”, “Night shift”, “Contractor crew A”).
Select the zones this badge may access.
Set time windows — always, or only during certain hours/days.
Optionally set an expiry (useful for visitors, contractors, trial members).
Optionally enable badge printing so a physical badge is printed on first check-in.

A role controls what you can do in the software (e.g. “manager can run reports”).

6.2 Self-registration — step by step
This is the default path. The person does everything themselves on any browser; no admin is involved.
The person opens the registration page and enters the organisation code, their name, and work email.
They verify their email with a one-time code (OTP) sent to their inbox.
They read the DPDP consent notice and tick the required consent boxes.
They enable their webcam and capture their face. The system checks the image is sharp, real, and shows a face.
The raw photo is deleted; only the 512-number vector is kept. The person receives a confirmation and a user ID.

DPDP at registration
Consent is collected before any face is captured — never after.
Four consent items are mandatory: purpose, sensitivity acknowledgement, rights awareness, free consent.
A non-biometric alternative (e.g. manual attendance) is always offered — consent can never be forced.

6.3 Admin-assisted enrollment — step by step
Used when the person cannot self-register. An administrator enrolls them in person, with the person physically present, and records consent on their behalf. This unlocks the hospital, prison, government, and religious verticals.
The admin opens Admin › Enroll and selects the person type (patient, inmate, citizen, devotee, visitor, guardian).
The admin fills in the person's details and the explicit purpose of enrollment.
The admin confirms four things: the person is present, the purpose was explained, the person consented verbally or in writing, and the admin takes responsibility.
The admin captures the person's face on the webcam. The same quality checks apply.
Optionally a signed consent form is scanned and attached. An enrollment record and consent reference are generated, with the admin's identity logged.

Audit trail for admin enrollment
Every admin-assisted enrollment records: which admin, which person, the stated purpose, the consent method, and the timestamp.
If an expiry is set (common for visitors and patients), the data auto-deletes on that date.
This satisfies the DPDP requirement that consent collected on someone's behalf is fully traceable.

6.4 Visitor temporary enrollment
For walk-ins who need access for only a few hours. No full account is created and no organisation code is needed.
Reception generates a QR code or temporary link for the visitor.
The visitor scans the QR at a reception kiosk or opens the link, enters their name and phone number.
Their face is captured and a temporary enrollment is created, valid for a set window (e.g. four hours).
When the window ends, the enrollment auto-expires and the face data is deleted. A full audit record is kept.

6.5 Bulk import (events, large rollouts)
When an entity already has a list of people — an event with 2,000 pre-registered delegates, or a school with 500 students — the list can be imported in one operation.
The admin uploads a CSV or Excel file with names, IDs, and emails.
The platform creates accounts for everyone in the list.
Each person completes face capture either at a registration desk (admin-assisted) or via a personal link (self-service).
Until face capture is complete, the account is “pending” and cannot check in.

8. DPDP Act 2026 compliance

8.1 The erasure cascade
When a person requests erasure, the platform performs a verified, atomic deletion across every store. This is one of the most important compliance features and works as follows:
The request is identity-verified (re-auth via OTP) before anything is deleted.
The request is logged in the audit trail before deletion begins.
The face vector (Milvus), face image (MinIO), and database records (PostgreSQL) are deleted in parallel.
Each deletion is independently verified — the system confirms each store now returns nothing.
The session cache is flushed, consent is revoked, a final audit entry is written, and the person receives an erasure certificate.

What is NOT deleted on erasure
Attendance logs are retained for 3 years where labour law requires it — but they are de-linked from biometric data.
The audit log of the erasure itself is retained — proving the erasure happened is itself a compliance requirement.
Everything that can identify the person biometrically is permanently destroyed.
